There is a question we hear almost every week at the centre, phrased a thousand ways: "How do I take care of myself when everyone counts on me?" It comes from people who hold a lot. A family, a team, ageing parents, friends who call when things go wrong. Reliable, active people, the ones you can count on. And who, over time, forget themselves.

## The trap of those who hold everything together

This profile has a strength: it keeps moving. It solves, anticipates, organises, and finds in being useful a real source of meaning. It also has a blind spot: its ability to keep going masks how empty it is. Nobody sees it stop, so nobody worries. Neither does the person.

The signs come through the body before they come through the mind. Nights get shorter. Patience wears thin. Thoughts loop in the evening. Listening to someone, even someone you love, takes effort. And the usual answer is to push a little harder: manage more, run more, sleep less. It keeps the illusion of control, for a while.

> We are often more attentive to the smallest warning light on our car than to our own body.
> -- Olivia De Stoop, founder of hoona

## It is not selfish

Many people in this situation have absorbed a simple rule: others first. Taking time for themselves then feels like a fault, or a luxury for later, when everything is calm. Everything is never calm.

We suggest another reading. Taking care of yourself is not a bracket stolen from others: it is what lets you keep being there for them without losing yourself along the way. A flat battery recharges no one. And what takes the most courage, for someone who holds everything, is not doing more. It is letting go, a little, and allowing yourself to be helped.

## Three gestures to begin with

- **An appointment with yourself, in the diary.** Not "when I have time": a date, a time, like for anyone else. A walk in the forest, a session at the Dome, a consultation. What is written down exists.
- **One question to ask yourself every evening.** What did my body need today, and did I give it? Not to judge yourself. To listen to the whispers before they become shouts.
- **One thing someone else can do.** Just one, this week. Delegating is not abandoning: it is making room.

Taking care of yourself is how you keep taking care of others. In that order.
